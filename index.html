Stakbit Care Notes

Reading Light Correctly, Not Just Estimating It

Most plant care instructions describe light in vague terms — "bright indirect light," "low light" — without explaining how to actually measure it in a real room. A simple, reliable trick: hold a hand about a foot above the leaves in the middle of the day. A sharp, defined shadow usually means direct or very bright light. A soft, fuzzy shadow means moderate indirect light. No visible shadow at all usually means the spot is too dim for most flowering or fast-growing species.

Stakbit tip: a plant stretching toward a window with unusually long gaps between leaves is a common sign it's not getting enough light, even if it still looks green and alive.

Light exposure also changes with the seasons, sometimes dramatically. A spot that was bright and sunny in summer can become quite dim by winter as the sun's angle drops — a common, often-missed reason a plant that thrived for months suddenly starts struggling.

Why Overwatering Kills More Plants Than Underwatering

It's a counterintuitive fact for most new plant owners: far more houseplants die from too much water than too little. Roots need oxygen as much as they need moisture, and soil that stays constantly wet effectively drowns them, creating the conditions for root rot — a slow, often invisible failure until the leaves start yellowing.

A simple habit avoids most of this: check the soil with a finger before watering, rather than watering on a fixed schedule. If the top inch or two is still damp, it usually isn't time yet, regardless of what a calendar reminder says.

Humidity Matters More Than Most People Assume

Many popular houseplants originate from tropical environments with humidity levels far higher than the average heated or air-conditioned home. Dry indoor air, especially common in winter, can cause crispy leaf edges and slowed growth even when light and watering are both handled correctly.

Grouping plants together, using a small humidifier, or simply avoiding spots directly next to heating vents can make a noticeable difference — often more than any amount of fertilizer or repotting.
